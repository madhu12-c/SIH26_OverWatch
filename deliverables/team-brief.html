Yash scoring · clustering runs & tunes
Gets

specs.json, embeddings.npy, purchases.csv, and schemas.py — which already tells you which fields are hard blockers and which are ignored. Do not hard-code field names anywhere; read them from the schema.

Does

Own the matching logic and prove it behaves. Three rules matter more than the weights:

  • Hard blockers veto. schemas.hard_fields(cat) — a mismatch on any of these is an instant zero, however well everything else lines up.
  • Ignored fields never count. schemas.ignored_fields(cat) — brand and part number. This is exactly what lets SKF and FAG resolve to one item.
  • Missing is not mismatched. One record silent on a field is absent information, not evidence of difference. No penalty, no credit.

Then clustering by union-find, and the hazard that comes with it: A matches B, B matches C, but A and C are unrelated — connected components still groups all three, and one bridging record can drag hundreds of unrelated items into one blob. Defend with a high threshold, a check that every pair inside a formed cluster scores well, and a size cap that routes oversized clusters to review.

At 100 records skip blocking entirely — all-pairs is 4,950 comparisons, instant. Write blocking later when the record count actually demands it.

Hands back

pair_scores.json and clusters.json, plus the count of merges blocked by each rule — that count is the safety interlock slide.

Done when

R00001 and R00002 merge. R00003 and R00004 never do, and the run says which field stopped it.

Isha dataset research research
Does

Find real refinery item names from public sources and put them in a spreadsheet. Your output goes straight into the code, so the format matters.

Where to look: the GeM catalogue (gem.gov.in), tender BOQs on eprocure.gov.in, and manufacturer catalogues — SKF, FAG, L&T Valves, Jindal Saw, Flexitallic, Wika.

What CPCL actually buys — it is a refinery, so weight the list that way: pipes, valves, flanges, gaskets, pumps, bearings, mechanical seals, motors, instrumentation, safety consumables. Not steel-plant or mining stock.

Columns (pipe-separate anything with multiple values):

item_id | category | noun | modifiers | uom | base_price | brands | part_numbers | vendors

ITM-016 | valve | VALVE | GLOBE|2 IN|300#|CS BODY|FLANGED | EA | 16400 | Audco|BDK | GL-2-300 | L&T Valves

Target 200–300 items. Fifty good ones beat three hundred guessed ones.

Also

Screenshot every real description you find. This is the single most valuable thing you produce. Our biggest demo risk is a judge from CPCL looking at the data and saying it does not look real. Screenshots of genuine GeM and tender text are the proof that our corruption patterns were observed, not invented.

Hands back

seed_items.csv and a folder of screenshots.

Meghna narrative · domain · presenting research
Does

Your output is not a document — it is your own understanding. On the day, the deck is not what convinces anyone; the answer to a follow-up question is.

Learn cold:

  • What a material master is, and what SAP MM tables hold (MARA, MAKT, MARC)
  • Why descriptions are abbreviated — the 40-character MAKTX limit
  • Why SS316 ≠ SS304 — molybdenum, corrosion resistance, and what fails without it
  • What a 150# / 300# pressure class means and why mixing them is dangerous
  • What Informatica, SAP MDG, Verdantis and PROSOL sell

Have an answer ready for all five:

  1. "Informatica already does this. Why you?" — they need all data centralised, and no CPSE will hand over its prices and vendor list. That is architectural, not a missing feature.
  2. "What if it merges the wrong thing?" — hard blockers, conservative thresholds, and a human approves anything uncertain. Then show the blocked list.
  3. "We have four lakh codes. How long?" — one AI call per record, once, cached. Roughly ₹17,000 of inference for a million records.
  4. "What if the AI hallucinates?" — it extracts attributes; it never decides a merge. Matching is arithmetic and every score is reproducible.
  5. "Why would CPSEs share data?" — they do not have to. Only encoded values cross the boundary.

Screen 02 in detail — build this one first and build it well

This screen is the argument. Everything else is context. The sequence has to land in this order, because the reveal only works if the audience first believes a fuzzy matcher would fail: 

1.  Two description cards appear, side by side.
    Deliberately hard to read. Almost no shared words.

2.  A text-similarity meter fills to 0.31 and stops, in red.
    Label: "a conventional fuzzy matcher stops here"
    PAUSE HERE. Let it sit. This beat is the setup.

3.  Specs slide in beneath each card, one row at a time,
    ~120ms apart. As each pair lands, a tick appears between them.
      iso_designation  6205  ✓  6205
      bore_mm            25  ✓  25
      od_mm              52  ✓  52
      width_mm           15  ✓  15
      seal_type         2RS  ✓  2RS

4.  Brand row lands LAST and greys out, struck through:
      brand             SKF  ⊗  FAG    ignored
    This single row is the whole technical idea. Give it a beat of its own.

5.  Final score counts up to 0.94, in green.

Motion notes

  • Use staggerChildren: 0.12 on the spec list. Do not animate all rows at once — the sequence is the explanation.
  • Ticks: scale 0 → 1.15 → 1, spring. Small overshoot, no bounce party.
  • Score counter: ease-out over ~800ms, tabular-nums so digits do not jitter.
  • Honour prefers-reduced-motion — render the end state instantly.
  • A replay button. Presenters re-run a moment. Do not make them refresh the page.

Mobile: stack, never shrink

The comparison is side-by-side on a laptop and stacked vertically on a phone — record A with its specs, then record B with its specs, with the match indicator running down the left edge. Two columns squeezed onto a 390px screen is unreadable, and the phone build is the backup that has to work when the laptop does not.
